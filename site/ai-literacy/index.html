AI Literacy & Professional Ethics: Student Resource Guide

Welcome to your comprehensive study resource. This guide is designed to support your learning across all modules of our AI Literacy and Professional Ethics curriculum. It contains core concepts, frameworks, templates, and summaries of Australian legislation to prepare you for both your coursework and future professional practice as IT professionals.

Module 1: AI Prompt Engineering & Capability Building

1.1 Fundamentals of Prompting

A prompt is the text or question you give to an Artificial Intelligence (AI) model [1]. It acts as the direct guide for the model's response; writing clear, specific prompts leads to higher-quality and more useful answers [1]. Think of prompt engineering as giving instructions to a highly capable assistant [1].

AI models respond to several basic styles of prompts, each suited to different tasks:

  1. Question Prompts
  2. Purpose: Used to explain, inform, clarify, start conversations, or build understanding [1].

  3. Examples:

    • "What are the advantages of renewable energy?" [1]
    • "Why is sleep important for learning?" [1]
    • "How does 3D printing work?" [1]

  4. Instruction Prompts

  5. Purpose: Direct, action-oriented prompts that tell the AI exactly what to do [2].

  6. Examples:

    • "Summarize this article in 3 bullet points." [2]
    • "Write a professional email to a new client." [2]
    • "Generate a list of 5 blog post ideas about healthy eating." [2]

  7. Creative Prompts

  8. Purpose: Inspire original, imaginative, or narrative responses, encouraging storytelling, brainstorming, or artistic expression [2].

  9. Examples:

    • "Write a short story about a robot who learns to paint." [2]
    • "Create a funny slogan for a coffee shop." [2]
    • "Invent a new holiday and describe how people celebrate it." [2]

  10. Role-Based Prompts

  11. Purpose: Instruct the AI to adopt a specific persona, role, or professional perspective to tailor the tone, language, and situational context of the output [3].
  12. Examples:
    • "You are a teacher. Explain fractions to a 10-year-old." [3]
    • "Act as a travel agent. Plan a 3-day trip to Tokyo." [3]
    • "You are a recruiter. Review this resume and suggest improvements." [3]

1.2 Adding Context and Constraints

To maximize the effectiveness of an AI response, prompts must move beyond vague queries by incorporating specific context (such as the target audience or background) and constraints (such as length limitations or format requirements) [5].

Comparative Examples: Vague vs. Specific Prompting

Topic Vague Prompt (Without Context/Constraints) Specific Prompt (With Context & Constraints) Key Parameters Added
Photosynthesis Explain photosynthesis. [5] Explain photosynthesis to a 10-year-old in under 100 words, using simple language and a fun analogy. [5] Context: Audience is a 10-year-old [5]
Constraints: Under 100 words, simple language, use of a fun analogy [5]
Baking Instructions on how to bake a chocolate cake. [5] Describe how to bake a chocolate cake for beginners using only 5 simple ingredients and clear step-by-step instructions. [5] Context: Target audience is beginners [5]
Constraints: Limit to 5 ingredients, require step-by-step format [5]

1.3 Advanced Prompting & Refinement

As you develop your capabilities, you will move from simple inputs to strategic, multi-turn interactions. Follow these five core prompting techniques to consistently generate professional outputs:

  1. Be Clear and Specific: Do not leave details to chance. Specify your precise needs (e.g., "Write a professional 3-sentence email to a client explaining that their order is delayed by two days.") [96].
  2. Assign a Role (Persona): Establish the perspective, tone, and domain expertise the AI should adopt right at the beginning of the prompt [96].
  3. Provide Context and Data: Feed the AI the "raw ingredients" (supporting data, articles, background information, or internal guidelines) it needs to perform the task accurately [96].
  4. Define the Output Format: Direct the structure of the response. Specify whether you want an email, a markdown table, a bulleted list, a technical report, or a summary slide layout [96].
  5. Iterate and Refine: Recognize that a first prompt is rarely perfect [96]. If the AI misses the mark, do not start a new session; instead, engage in iterative prompting by providing constructive "follow-up" feedback to guide the model toward the desired outcome [96].

Prompting Limitations

While Generative AI is a powerful tool, it exhibits clear boundaries that require human judgment to manage [98]: * Knowledge Cutoff: The model's training data has a temporal limit; it cannot access real-time events past this cutoff unless equipped with live search tools [98]. * Quality Dependence: The output is directly tied to input quality; poor, vague prompts inevitably produce low-quality results ("garbage in, garbage out") [98]. * Hallucination: AI can confidently state incorrect or fabricated facts, dates, names, or citations [11, 43, 98]. * Bias Risks: Models can mirror, amplify, or reinforce systemic biases present in their training datasets [98]. * Inconsistency: Outputs can vary unpredictably, meaning the same prompt may generate different responses across sessions [98].

Human Oversight Rule: AI tools are powerful assistants, but they require critical human judgment to verify factual outputs, apply contextual understanding, and make final decisions [98].

Module 2: Information Literacy, Verification, & Deep Research

2.1 Deep Research vs. Standard LLMs

Understanding the technological capabilities of your tools is essential for conducting professional research.

  • Deep Research: Refers to multi-step, autonomous investigations conducted by specialized AI agents across the internet or databases to answer complex, multi-layered questions [9].
  • Standard LLM: Focuses on text generation, summarization, and direct question answering utilizing its static, pre-trained internal knowledge base [11].

Deep Research vs. Regular LLM

Feature / Dimension AI Deep Research Platforms Regular LLM Systems
Core Goal To investigate, analyze, and synthesize detailed information by pulling dynamically from external data sources or combining multiple models [11]. To generate text, translate languages, summarize documents, or answer routine queries using pre-trained internal parameters [11].
Primary Data Source Real-time web-scraping, live search queries, and external scholarly/industry databases [9, 11]. Static pre-training dataset frozen at a specific point in time (Knowledge Cutoff) [11, 98].
Handling Hallucinations Minimizes factual gaps by verifying claims across multiple independent online sources, cross-checking data points, and citing direct URLs [11, 16]. More prone to "hallucinating" plausible-sounding but completely fabricated facts, statistics, or citations when internal data is missing [11].

Understanding AI Hallucinations

As an IT professional, you must be alert to the four main types of hallucinations: 1. Factual Errors: Presenting incorrect details such as wrong dates, historical events, or names [11]. 2. Invented Citations: Fabricating academic papers, titles, URLs, or authors that do not exist [11]. 3. Contradictory Statements: Asserting a claim in one paragraph and contradicting it in the next [11]. 4. Contextual Misinterpretations: Reading correct data but misapplying it to an unrelated context or drawing illogical conclusions [11].

Verifying Sources: Key Definitions

To confidently sort sources, look for these two professional vetting mechanisms: * Peer-Reviewed Publication: Work that has been rigorously vetted, reviewed, and critiqued by independent experts in the same academic or scientific field before being published online [35]. This process ensures the methodology is scientifically valid, of high quality, and highly credible [35]. * Editorial Standards: A defined set of rules, policies, and ethical guidelines used by professional publications to ensure content is accurate, fair, consistent, and professional [35]. These standards maintain organizational integrity through mandatory fact-checking, clear writing, source protection, and harm avoidance [35].

2.3 Strategic Research Techniques

To research efficiently, select the appropriate search methodology and tools:

1. Search Operators for Web Engines

  • Quotation Marks ("..."): Force search engines to find exact phrases [86]. Use when searching for specific policy names, laws, or technical definitions.
  • Minus Symbol (-): Exclude specific words from search results [86]. (e.g., searching AI recruitment -sales to filter out marketing pitches).
  • Advanced Search Filters: Utilize search engine menus to narrow results by region, domain (such as .gov.au or .edu.au), or file type (such as .pdf).

2. The "Research Lens"

  • Zoom Out (Look Wide): Spend time scanning a broad landscape of articles to establish a high-level overview of a topic, mapping out key subtopics or emerging trends [55].
  • Zoom In (Dive Deep): Select a highly specific, credible source or technical case study and read deeply to build a thorough, granular understanding of the mechanics, challenges, and solutions [55].

Module 3: Legal & Ethical Frameworks in AI

3.1 Responsible AI Frameworks

Implementing AI requires balancing innovation with ethical guardrails. Two primary frameworks guide this balance in the Australian IT sector:

1. BKI's S.A.F.E.-A.I. Framework

This framework outlines organizational responsibilities for deploying AI technologies safely [98]: * S = Secure Data Practices: Implement robust encryption, secure storage, and strict access controls to protect all data ingested or generated by AI [98]. * A = Accountability: Establish clear channels of human ownership; humans are responsible for the outcomes and decisions of AI tools [98]. * F = Fairness: Regularly test models for bias to prevent discriminatory outcomes against specific demographic groups [98]. * E = Ethical Integrity: Ensure AI usage aligns with organizational values, social responsibility, and human rights [98]. * A = Accuracy & Validation: Establish continuous testing regimes to verify AI outputs, mitigate hallucinations, and ensure reliable performance [98]. * I = Innovation with Oversight: Foster technological growth and creative AI usage, but always maintain strict human review and operational guardrails [99].

2. Australia’s 8 AI Ethics Principles

Published by the Department of Industry, Science and Resources, these national principles assist organizations in building and utilizing AI responsibly [69, 71]: 1. Human, societal and environmental wellbeing: AI systems should benefit individuals, society, and the environment [69]. 2. Human-centred values: AI systems should respect human rights, diversity, and the autonomy of individuals [69]. 3. Fairness: AI systems should be inclusive and accessible, and should not involve or result in unfair discrimination against individuals, communities, or groups [69]. 4. Privacy protection and security: AI systems should respect and uphold privacy rights and data protection, and ensure the security of data [69]. 5. Reliability and safety: AI systems should reliably operate in accordance with their intended purpose [69]. 6. Transparency and explainability: There should be transparency and responsible disclosure so people can understand when they are being significantly impacted by AI, and can find out when an AI system is engaging with them [69]. 7. Contestability: When an AI system significantly impacts a person, community, group, or environment, there should be a timely process to allow people to challenge the use or outcomes of the AI system [69]. 8. Accountability: People responsible for the different phases of the AI system lifecycle should be identifiable and accountable for the outcomes of the AI systems, and human oversight of AI systems should be enabled [69].

3.2 AI and Anti-Discrimination Law

AI systems do not operate in a legal vacuum. If an AI tool produces biased outcomes, the organization deploying it can be held legally liable under Australian law.

Defining Bias vs. Discrimination

  • Bias in AI: A systematic, non-random error or prejudice built into the software's algorithm or training data that causes unfair, inconsistent outcomes [67]. It is distinct from random technical glitches because it consistently and predictably disadvantages specific groups [67].
  • Discrimination: Treating an individual or group unfairly or differently because of personal characteristics that are protected by law [75].

Common Types of Algorithmic Bias [67]

  • Historical Bias: Training an AI model on historical datasets that reflect past human prejudices or societal inequalities, causing the AI to reproduce those same inequalities [67, 84].
  • Representation Bias: Occurs when the training dataset fails to adequately represent the diversity of the actual population, leading to poorer performance for underrepresented groups [67].
  • Measurement Bias: Occurs when the data collected for training does not accurately measure the real-world characteristics it is intended to capture [67].

Key Australian Anti-Discrimination Legislation

As an IT professional, you must ensure that your systems do not violate federal anti-discrimination laws [75]: * Racial Discrimination Act 1975: Prohibits discrimination based on race, color, descent, national origin, or ethnic origin [75]. * Sex Discrimination Act 1984: Protects individuals from discrimination based on sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, intersex status, pregnancy, potential pregnancy, breastfeeding, or family responsibilities [75]. * Disability Discrimination Act 1992: Prohibits discrimination against people with physical, intellectual, psychiatric, sensory, neurological, or learning disabilities [75]. * Age Discrimination Act 2004: Protects both younger and older Australians from age-based discrimination in employment, education, and services [75]. * Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986: Establishes the Commission and details broad protections for human rights in federal jurisdiction [75].

3.3 Privacy Legislation & Personal Information

Handling data responsibly is the core technical duty of an IT professional.

Defining Personal vs. Sensitive Information

  • Personal Information: Information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable [77]. Examples include names, dates of birth, physical addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, employment history, and educational qualifications [77].
  • Sensitive Information: A highly protected subset of personal information [77]. It includes information about an individual's health, disability status, racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, membership in professional/trade unions, sexual orientation, or criminal records [77].

The Privacy Act 1988 & APPs

The Privacy Act 1988 is the primary federal law regulating how Australian government agencies and private sector organizations (with some exceptions) handle personal information [78]. It is centered around the 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) [78]. When implementing AI, pay close attention to these key principles [78]:

  • APP 1 - Open and Transparent Management of Personal Information: Organizations must have a clear, up-to-date, and accessible privacy policy detailing how they manage personal information [78, 85].
  • APP 3 - Collection of Solicited Personal Information: You must only collect personal information that is reasonably necessary for your organization's direct functions, and sensitive information must only be collected with explicit consent [78, 79].
  • APP 5 - Notification of Collection: At or before the time of collection, organizations must take reasonable steps to notify individuals that their data is being collected, why it is being collected, and how it will be used (e.g., notifying candidates if their resume is being analyzed by an AI screening tool) [78, 85].
  • APP 6 - Use or Disclosure of Personal Information: Data collected for a specific purpose cannot be used or disclosed for another purpose without the individual's consent, unless a legal exception applies [78].
  • APP 8 - Cross-Border Disclosure: Before disclosing personal information to an overseas recipient (such as storing candidate profile data on an overseas cloud server), the Australian organization must take reasonable steps to ensure the overseas recipient complies with the APPs. The Australian organization remains legally accountable for any privacy breaches overseas [78, 79, 85].
  • APP 11 - Security of Personal Information: Organizations must protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, unauthorized access, modification, or disclosure [78].
  • APP 12 - Access to Personal Information: Individuals have a right to access the personal information held about them by an organization [78].
  • APP 13 - Correction of Personal Information: Individuals have a right to request the correction of inaccurate, out-of-date, incomplete, irrelevant, or misleading personal information held about them [79].

3.4 Vendor Instructions & Liability

When acquiring and integrating AI tools from external software providers (vendors), IT staff must carefully analyze the accompanying Vendor Instructions [80].

What Are Vendor Instructions?

These are formal, system-specific documents provided by the technology supplier, including [80]: * Software User Manuals: Explaining how the system operates and its logical workflows [80]. * System Configuration Guides: Outlining the technical setup, installation parameters, and system requirements [80]. * API Documentation: Detailing how other software systems interface and share data with the AI tool [80]. * Security Settings Recommendations: Instructions on data encryption, user permissions, and cloud firewall rules [80]. * Data Formatting Requirements: Guidelines on how input data must be cleaned, structured, and labeled to be processed correctly by the model [80].

The Accountability Principle

While vendor instructions are essential for technical implementation, they carry a major legal limitation:

The Compliance Rule: Following vendor instructions does not override your legal obligations [80]. If an AI system violates Australian anti-discrimination, privacy, or consumer protection laws, the organization deploying the tool is held legally responsible [79]. You cannot shift legal liability to the software vendor or use "the algorithm made the decision" as a defense [79]. Humans always remain legally responsible for AI outcomes [81].

Module 4: Practical Project Scenario & Digital Skills

4.1 Project Case Study: TalentMatch Solutions

To apply these legal, ethical, and prompting concepts, you will analyze the fictional case study of TalentMatch Solutions, a professional recruitment agency specializing in placing candidates in technology roles [82].

The Implementation

To improve efficiency, candidate matching, and delivery times, TalentMatch Solutions implemented an AI-powered resume screening system [82, 83]. It was trained on the company's historical hiring data from the past five years [84].

The Three Post-Implementation Failures

After three months of live operation, several serious issues emerged, reflecting real-world failures in AI management [83]:

  1. Bias Issues [84]
  2. University Bias: The AI consistently ranked candidates from specific universities higher, completely ignoring relevant qualifications from other institutions [84].
  3. Employment Gaps: Candidates who had employment gaps on their resumes (due to taking parental leave or recovering from health issues) were systematically downgraded by the algorithm [84].
  4. Gender Disparity: Female candidates with equivalent qualifications progressed through the screening pipeline at significantly lower rates than male candidates [84].

  5. The Cause: The system was trained on historical hiring data, meaning it inherited, codified, and automated the historic human biases of recruiters from the previous 5 years [84].

  6. Privacy Concerns [84, 85]

  7. Sensitive Data Ingestion: The AI collected detailed personal profiles, including highly sensitive medical data regarding candidates' physical or mental disabilities, without clear justification [84].
  8. Cross-Border Disclosure: Candidate data was uploaded and stored on overseas cloud servers without verifying compliance or conducting impact assessments [85].
  9. Lack of Transparency: Candidates were never informed that their professional profiles and careers were being screened by an automated AI system [85].

  10. Regulatory Failure: The company's privacy policy had not been updated to reflect the use of AI, and no mechanism existed for candidates to access, correct, or request the deletion of their personal information [85].

  11. Workplace and Operational Issues [85]

  12. Depersonalization: Internal recruiters felt the hiring process had become entirely automated, mechanical, and depersonalized, stripping away human connection [85].
  13. Client Resistance: Several key corporate clients specifically requested "no AI involvement" in their hiring contracts [85].
  14. Lack of Trust: Internal recruitment staff did not trust the recommendations generated by the AI, leading to friction and low adoption [85].
  15. Operational Overhead: Any time saved by automated screening was completely offset by the hours staff spent troubleshooting, correcting algorithmic errors, and managing candidate complaints [85].
  16. Financial Friction: Senior management was frustrated by the lack of clear financial return on investment (ROI) from the expensive software deployment [85].

4.2 Collaborative Practice & Rehearsal Tools

Success in modern IT environments requires a mix of technical literacy and collaborative communication skills.

1. PowerPoint Presenter Coach

For your group presentations, utilize Microsoft PowerPoint's Rehearse with Coach feature (located under the Slide Show tab) to practice and refine your delivery [13, 117]. The built-in AI assistant actively listens to your presentation and provides an immediate, interactive dashboard tracking five metrics: * Pace: Monitors your talking speed. The ideal target is around 120 to 130 words per minute. It alerts you if you are speaking too fast (due to nerves) or too slow (monotonous). * Fillers: Pinpoints non-lexical filler words (such as "umm", "uhh", "like", or "you know") and suggests pausing to take a breath instead of filling the silence. * Pitch: Warns you if your voice falls into a flat, monotone pattern, urging you to vary your pitch to keep the audience engaged. * Originality: Detects if you are simply reading the text on your slides aloud, coaching you to explain key points in your own words. * Repetitive Language: Tracks word usage and flags when you repeat phrases excessively, suggesting synonyms to keep your delivery fresh and professional.

2. Digital Collaboration Tools

When working in teams to compile research and complete group tasks, utilize these essential tools: * Miro: A virtual interactive whiteboard. Excellent for mind mapping, mapping out software integrations, and brainstorming layout strategies [45]. * Padlet: A collaborative digital canvas. Use to post quick research findings, share relevant web links, and brainstorm answers during group class sessions [14, 45]. * Teams / Zoom / Google Meet: Secure video conferencing platforms to hold remote team syncs, record presentations, and establish collaborative plans [13]. * Google Docs / Microsoft Office Online: Platforms for co-authoring presentation materials, sharing documents, and tracking team edit histories [14, 45].

3. Staying Current with AI

The rapid pace of AI development means your knowledge must be continuously refreshed [57]. Build professional habits to keep up-to-date: * Professional Communities: Join technical LinkedIn groups, follow sector leaders, and participate in online tech forums [88]. * Media and Video: Subscribe to reputable, expert-led tech channels on YouTube to watch platform demonstrations and breakdown tutorials [88]. * Podcasts: Listen to leading AI-focused podcasts (such as The Next Wave with Matt Wolfe and Nathan Lands) to stay updated on emerging models (like Gemini 3) and sector implementations [88, 91].
